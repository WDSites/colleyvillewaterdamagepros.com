Why air conditioners leak so often in a Texas summer

A cooling system pulls moisture out of the indoor air, and that moisture has to go somewhere. On a hot North Texas afternoon the coil can condense several gallons of water over a day. A small drain line carries it outside or to a plumbing connection, and when that line clogs, the water has nowhere to go but the pan, the floor or the ceiling.

This is one of the most common summer causes of indoor water damage in our area, and it is usually slow. Nobody sees a flood. Instead, a drip wets insulation, drywall and framing a little more each day, and the first sign is often a stain that appears after the damage is already well along.

Air handlers sit in attics, closets, garages and utility rooms, so the wet area is often out of sight. A unit in the attic can soak the ceiling below it. A closet unit can wick water into the carpet and the wall behind it.

Common ways the leak starts

  • Clogged condensate drain. Algae, dust and debris build into a slime that blocks the small line, so water backs up into the pan and spills over.
  • Cracked or rusted drain pan. Older pans corrode, and a hairline crack leaks quietly into whatever sits below.
  • Frozen evaporator coil. Low airflow or low refrigerant ices the coil, and when the ice melts it can overload the pan all at once.
  • Failed float switch or loose fittings. The safety switch that should shut the unit off does not trip, or a joint in the line drips.
  • Sweating ducts. Cold ducts in a hot attic collect condensation and drip onto insulation and ceilings.

Safety steps before you touch a leaking AC system

Electricity and a wet ceiling do not mix

The air handler, its wiring and nearby light fixtures may be wet. If water is dripping through a light fitting or near an outlet, stay out of that room until the power is off.

  • Turn the thermostat to off, then switch off the breaker for the air handler.
  • If the ceiling is bulging, shut off the breaker for that room and keep people from standing under it.
  • Do not poke a bulging ceiling with your hand, because the water behind it may be heavy and dirty.
  • Never climb into a wet attic in the dark or step between joists you cannot see.

How crews dry and repair the damage

Crews start by finding where the water went, which is rarely just the spot with the stain. A moisture meter and a thermal camera show wet drywall, insulation and framing around and above the visible mark. Wet insulation in the attic usually has to come out, because it holds water against the ceiling and dries slowly.

Drying then uses air movers and a dehumidifier sized to the space, and crews check the readings on return visits rather than guessing. ANSI/IICRC S500 is the reference standard for this kind of water damage work. Drywall that is sagging, stained through or still wet after drying is cut out and replaced.

The split of responsibility is simple. Our crews dry and repair the water damage. An HVAC technician clears the drain, replaces the pan or fixes the coil, and no amount of drying prevents a repeat leak if the equipment problem stays. If the unit has to be replaced, Building Inspections at 817-503-1030 handles permit questions for Colleyville, and the technician can say whether one applies.

When the damage is bigger than one room, the work becomes water mitigation across several areas. If it ends with new drywall, paint and insulation, that rebuild falls under water damage repair.

Humidity, odor and the mold question

Federal EPA guidance sets a drying window of one to two days before mold becomes likely, and it wants indoor humidity held under 60 percent, with 30 to 50 percent as the ideal band. AC leaks defeat that target because the soaked material sits in a warm, dark attic or closet. After weeks of dripping, growth may already coat the back of the drywall.

If you see dark spotting or smell something earthy after the leak, do not scrub it or spray it. Disturbing it spreads spores. Under TDLR rules, testing and removal must be done by separate licensed firms on any one job. Our mold remediation page lays out how that split plays out.

What changes the cost of an AC leak job

  • How long the leak ran, since weeks of dripping soak far more material than an overnight overflow.
  • Where the water went: attic insulation, a ceiling, a wall cavity, flooring or all of them.
  • Whether drywall can dry in place or has to be cut out and replaced.
  • Attic access, which affects how crews carry in equipment and remove wet insulation.
  • Finish work after drying, such as texture, paint and flooring edges.

We do not quote numbers over the phone before anyone has seen the damage. The dispatcher asks about the unit, the stain and how long it has been going, then a licensed and insured crew gives a scope after reading the moisture.

Is an AC leak covered by homeowners insurance?

It depends on how the water got out. Per the state insurance department, a policy typically responds to a sudden, accidental release and typically does not respond to slow seepage, so a drain that clogged and dripped for weeks may fall outside. Report hidden damage promptly, protect the property, and let the adjuster look before anything permanent gets built. Call us and we will document what we find.

Mistakes that make an AC leak worse

  • Running the system again to see whether it still leaks, which can send more water through the ceiling.
  • Pouring bleach down the drain line without asking the HVAC technician, since the wrong treatment can damage the pan or coil.
  • Painting over a ceiling stain, which hides the sign without drying what is behind it.
  • Ignoring a small spot because the weather cooled off and the dripping stopped.
  • Skipping the yearly drain line check before summer starts.
