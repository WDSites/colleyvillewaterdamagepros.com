Basements here are the exception

Basements are not the common case in Colleyville, and we have no source that gives a count of homes with one. Most local houses sit on a slab at ground level, so the water problems we hear about most are roof leaks, burst pipes and storm water at the doors. For rising water from a storm, our flood damage restoration page is the better starting point.

This page covers the homes that do have a lower level. That includes true basements, walkout levels cut into a slope, finished below-grade rooms and cellars under additions. If you have one of those, the water behaves differently than it does in a slab house.

How water enters a below-grade room

Water works its way down, and a below-grade room is the lowest place it can go. DFW's normal rain total is 37.01 inches a year according to the Fort Worth weather service, and a heavy storm can saturate the soil beside a lower wall. Colleyville's own stormwater page puts the start of flash flooding near an inch of rainfall in a single hour, a rate yard drains often cannot keep up with.

  • Groundwater seepage. Wet soil pushes water through wall cracks, floor joints and the seam where the wall meets the floor.
  • Window wells. A well that fills with rain and has no working drain sends water over the sill and into the room.
  • Walkout doors. Water runs down a sloped patio toward the door and under the threshold.
  • Sump pump failure. A pump that loses power in a storm or has a stuck float lets the pit overflow.
  • Plumbing. A water heater, supply line or washer in the lower level leaks with nowhere for the water to go.
  • Sewer or drain backup. Floor drains in a lower level are the first place a blocked line shows itself.

Safety steps for a flooded lower level

Water and electricity in a low room

A lower level often has outlets, a panel, a furnace or a water heater close to the floor. Standing water that touches any of these can energize the water.

  • Do not step into standing water if outlets, cords or appliances are in it.
  • If the panel is in the wet room, call the utility or an electrician before touching it.
  • Shut off the main water valve if a pipe or fixture is the source.
  • Stay out of rooms where the water is deep or rising, because stairs can be slick and the pressure on a door can hold it shut.
  • Do not run a household vacuum on water.

A water emergency on the city side of the meter after hours goes to on-call Public Works at 817.743.4522. For a flooded room, call us and we arrange the crew. Closing the main valve also protects a later claim, since insurers can refuse owners who let water keep running.

How crews remove water and dry a lower level

  1. 1

    Extract the water

    Submersible pumps and truck-mounted or portable extractors remove standing water. See emergency water removal for that stage in detail.

  2. 2

    Sort what stays and what goes

    Carpet, pad, wet drywall and stored cardboard are checked. Contaminated or saturated porous items are removed.

  3. 3

    Map the moisture

    A meter and an infrared camera reveal how far water crept into wall bases, framing and the slab.

  4. 4

    Dry the structure

    Drying equipment keeps running until the meters read normal again, and crews return to recheck.

  5. 5

    Treat and clean

    Surfaces are cleaned, and growth that has started is flagged for a licensed mold professional.

Concrete holds water far longer than drywall, and below-grade air is cool and still, so drying takes patience. The ANSI/IICRC S500 document, used here only as a reference, describes the water categories and drying methods. How long it takes depends on the materials, the amount of water and the conditions in the room.

Humidity and mold in a below-grade space

Mold becomes likely when wet materials stay damp past roughly two days, and the EPA suggests holding indoor humidity under 60 percent. Lower levels start at a disadvantage because cool concrete walls draw in damp air. A dehumidifier may be needed long after the water is gone.

If growth shows up on drywall, framing or stored items, see our mold remediation page. Texas licenses these trades, and the assessor and the remediator on a project must be different firms.

What changes the cost of a basement job

  • Depth and volume of standing water.
  • The category of water, since sewage or contaminated water requires more removal.
  • Finished versus unfinished space, because carpet, drywall and trim add material to dry or replace.
  • How long the water sat before anyone called.
  • Stairs, tight doorways and distance from the street, which affect equipment handling.
  • Whether the source is plumbing, drainage or storm water.

Census figures put the typical owner-occupied home here at roughly three quarters of a million dollars or more, so a finished lower level often carries real finish work. Nobody can scope the job until the water and the walls have been checked on site.

Does insurance cover a wet basement?

Standard home policies leave out flooding, which takes a separate policy. A sudden plumbing failure is usually covered, while slow seepage usually is not. Ask your insurer about a sump pump or water backup endorsement before you need it, and ask whether your loss counts as flood or plumbing. The city's Engineering Division administers floodplains, and the FEMA flood map at msc.fema.gov is worth a look. We document the damage when you call.

Mistakes to avoid and ways to prevent repeats

  • Opening a door under pressure to let water out, which can push a rush of water into the room.
  • Leaving carpet and pad down after a soaking.
  • Storing valuables in cardboard directly on the floor.
  • Letting the sump pump run without a test or a battery backup.
  • Piling mulch or soil above the window well.
