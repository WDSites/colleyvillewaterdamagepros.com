How a supply pipe lets go inside a Colleyville house

A supply line is under constant pressure, so a split does not seep. It sprays or pours until someone closes a valve. Colleyville runs its own water utility, and the city's published budget shows its treated supply is bought from the Trinity River Authority. By the time that water reaches your meter it is pressurized and will keep flowing until a valve stops it.

Pipes fail for ordinary reasons. Old copper pinholes, a corroded fitting, a braided hose that was twisted at install, a nail through a line, or a joint that worked loose over years of heating and cooling. Texas also has its share of hard freezes, and a split from ice is a separate problem we cover on the frozen pipe water damage page.

Where the pipe sits changes the damage. A break in an attic soaks insulation and then drops water through the ceiling. A break inside a wall runs down the cavity and wicks into the baseboard and carpet. A break under a sink fills the cabinet base first and then creeps onto the floor.

What you see and what it usually means

What you noticeWhat it usually points to

A spinning meter is a sign worth acting on even when you cannot see any water yet.

Surface signs understate the loss. Drywall can look fine on the face while the back is saturated, and insulation holds water like a sponge. That is why a moisture meter and a thermal camera matter more than your eyes.

First-hour safety steps

Do these in order before anything else

Water and electricity do not mix. If water is coming through a ceiling light, near outlets or across a panel, stay out of that area and treat the circuit as live.

  • Close the main shut-off valve. Turn it fully until the flow stops, then open a low faucet to drain the lines.
  • If you can reach the breaker box without stepping in water, switch off power to the wet rooms.
  • Do not use a household vacuum on standing water or step on a sagging ceiling.
  • Move small furniture, rugs and electronics to a dry room if it is safe to do so.
  • Photograph the damage before you move much, then call (817) 623-9267.

If you cannot find or turn the main valve, the City of Colleyville lists an after-hours water emergency number for on-call Public Works at 817.743.4522. That crew works on the city side of the meter. They will not dry your house, so call the dispatch line for that part.

What the cleanup and drying look like

  1. 1

    Locate and confirm the source

    The break is found and the line is isolated so a repair plumber can work. Dispatch cannot fix the pipe itself, so plan on a licensed plumber for that.

  2. 2

    Remove standing water

    Crews use pumps and wet vacuums to take off pooled water. Extraction in depth is on the emergency water removal page.

  3. 3

    Open and cut what cannot dry

    Saturated insulation, ceiling drywall that sags and carpet pad are usually pulled out. Flood cuts a few inches above the wet line let air reach the cavity.

  4. 4

    Set drying equipment

    Air movers push air over wet surfaces and LGR dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the room. Some jobs add wall cavity drying or floor drying systems.

  5. 5

    Monitor until the readings match dry

    A crew returns, records readings and moves or removes equipment. The reference for the method is ANSI/IICRC S500, the water damage restoration standard.

EPA guidance sets the drying target at 24 to 48 hours, because mold gets a foothold after that. A pipe break that runs overnight can already be near the edge of that window by morning, which is why a late-night call is reasonable. If growth has started, our mold remediation page explains what that work involves.

What changes the cost

  • How much water escaped and how long it ran before the valve was closed.
  • Which materials got wet. Drywall and carpet pad are cheaper to replace than hardwood or cabinets.
  • Whether the water was clean supply water or had crossed dirty surfaces.
  • How many rooms and floors the water traveled through, including an attic or a lower level.
  • How many days of drying equipment and monitoring the structure needs.
  • Whether finishes must be rebuilt afterward, which is covered by water damage repair.

We do not quote a number over the phone because the readings decide the scope. A crew should walk the house, explain what is wet and why, and put the plan in writing before the work grows.

Burst pipes and your policy

The state insurance department draws a line between a sudden failure, like a pipe that bursts, and a slow leak that goes on for weeks. Standard homeowner and renter policies tend to respond to the first and not the second. TDI also warns that a claim can be denied if the owner left the main valve open. Photograph everything, keep damaged items for the adjuster, and call your insurer before permanent repairs begin.

Burst pipe mistakes that make the cleanup harder

  • Waiting for daylight. Water keeps moving into framing while you decide, and drying clocks keep running.
  • Fixing the pipe and stopping there. A repaired line does nothing for a wet wall cavity, and a patched ceiling hides a damp attic.
  • Using space heaters to dry a wall. Heat on wet drywall and carpet can speed odor and does not reach the cavity.
  • Throwing everything out unseen. The adjuster may want to see the pipe section and the damaged materials.
  • Ignoring a leak that stops by itself. Water already inside walls stays there and feeds mold.

To lower the odds of a next one, replace aged braided hoses, learn where the main valve is and test it twice a year, and look under sinks when you do other chores. A water alarm placed near the heater and the washer costs little and gives an early warning.
