What a sewage backup is and why it happens

A sewage backup is wastewater pushed back up through the lowest openings in a house. That means floor drains, shower and tub drains, toilets and sometimes a laundry standup pipe. The water carries human waste, soap, grease and whatever else went down the line, so restoration trade practice treats it as the most contaminated class of water, called Category 3.

The most common cause is a blockage somewhere in the line that leaves the house. Grease, wipes, tree roots and a collapsed section of pipe can all stop the flow. Water then looks for the first way out, and the first way out is usually the lowest fixture in the home.

Colleyville homes are mostly large, newer-style houses on slabs with fixtures at ground level, so a backup tends to spread across tile and wood floors quickly. No source tells us how often backups happen locally, and we will not guess. What we can say is that once it starts, the water spreads fast and the contamination goes with it.

Dark water with a strong odor on tile or woodCategory 3 water that has already reached flooring and baseboards

A single slow sink is usually a local clog. Several fixtures acting up at once suggests the main line.

Dark water, odor and rising levels all say the same thing: stop using water and treat the room as contaminated. A plumber clears the line. Restoration crews handle what the water touched.

First-hour safety steps for a sewage backup

Health and electrical safety first

Sewage can carry bacteria and viruses, and contact through cuts, eyes or the mouth is the main risk. Do not wade through it barefoot, do not run a household vacuum on it and do not let children or pets into the room.

  • Stop using sinks, showers, toilets, the dishwasher and the washing machine so more water is not added to the line.
  • If water has reached outlets or appliances, cut power to that area, but touch the panel only when you can stand on dry ground.
  • Keep people and pets out of the affected rooms and close doors to limit spread.
  • Wash hands thoroughly with soap and clean water after any contact, and bag soiled clothing separately.
  • Take photos from the doorway before anything is moved.

Your line or the city main: who to call

A blockage can sit in the pipe on your property or in the public main that serves the street. We cannot tell you which side it is, and we will not claim ownership of either. The city and your plumber can sort that out, and you should ask the city directly.

The City of Colleyville provides wastewater service. Its published figures describe roughly 173 miles of wastewater mains, 2,690 manholes and 3 pumping facilities, and those counts may change. For a suspected main problem, the City's Field Operations team lists 817.503.1360. If neighbors on the street have the same trouble, say so when you call.

While you wait, a plumber with a camera can show where the stoppage is. That video also helps if you later talk to your insurer or the city, so keep a copy.

How crews clean up after sewage

Crews work in protective gear that usually includes gloves, boots, eye protection and a respirator, and they set up so contamination does not travel to clean rooms. Wet floors are extracted first, then the remaining liquid is picked up and bagged for disposal.

  1. 1

    Contain and extract

    Doors are closed or sealed with plastic, and a pump or extractor removes standing wastewater.

  2. 2

    Remove what cannot be cleaned

    Carpet, pad, wet drywall, insulation and porous trim that absorbed sewage are cut out and bagged. Porous material rarely returns to a safe condition.

  3. 3

    Clean and disinfect

    Hard surfaces are scrubbed with a detergent, rinsed and then treated with an antimicrobial product. Floor drains and nearby cabinet bases get extra attention.

  4. 4

    Dry out the rooms

    Fans and dehumidifiers pull out leftover dampness, and meter readings confirm the result before any rebuilding.

  5. 5

    Document and hand off

    Photos and notes are kept for your claim file, and the remaining repairs move to water damage repair.

The industry reference for these steps is ANSI/IICRC S500, which describes how contaminated water should be handled. We name it as a reference, not as a certification claim. Drying methods are explained in more detail on the structural drying page.

What changes the cost of sewage cleanup

Price depends on how far the water traveled, how long it sat and what it soaked. A bathroom floor drain overflow is a very different job from a backup that reached a hallway and two bedrooms. Each item below moves the scope.

  • Area reached by the water and the number of rooms involved
  • Flooring type, since carpet and pad are removed while sealed tile can often be cleaned
  • Whether wall bases, cabinets or insulation absorbed the water
  • Depth of the backup and how long it stood before cleanup began
  • How much drying and demolition the structure needs

A written scope, reviewed before work starts, is the best way to keep the job predictable. Nothing in this section is a quote, and the dispatch line cannot give a figure over the phone.

Does insurance cover a sewage backup?

Coverage varies a great deal. Per TDI guidance, a standard home policy leaves out flooding, which takes a separate flood policy. A sewage backup from a drain is a plumbing event, not a flood, so ask your insurer whether you have a drain or sewer backup endorsement. We do not claim that your policy pays, and we cannot promise how a claim will go.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Running more water to see if the drain clears, which adds to the flooding
  • Cleaning with a household wet vacuum, which can spread the contamination through the exhaust
  • Keeping wet carpet or pad because it looks recoverable
  • Pouring chemical drain openers into a backed-up line, which can hurt a plumber who opens it later
  • Throwing out proof before photos are taken

If a backup happened once, ask a plumber about the line. Repeat events often point to roots or a failing section of pipe, and cleanup alone will not fix that.
