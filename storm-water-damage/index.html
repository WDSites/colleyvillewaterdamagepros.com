How storms let water into Colleyville houses

Storm water damage is wet damage that starts from above or from the side. Hail breaks shingles, wind lifts flashing, a fallen limb punches through decking, and driven rain finds seams around windows and doors. The water then drops into the attic, runs along framing and shows up as stains or drips below.

National Weather Service Fort Worth data show that big hail, damaging wind, flooding and tornadoes strike somewhere in the Metroplex almost every year. Severe weather is most frequent in spring. A storm loss is a normal possibility for a local homeowner, not a freak event.

The April 11, 2016 hailstorm produced a 5.25 inch stone, an NWS Fort Worth area record, though the worst damage that day was in Wylie and not in Tarrant County. We mention it to show how large hail can get in this region, not to claim that it struck Colleyville.

Damage that looks small from the ground can be larger above the ceiling. A stain the size of a dinner plate sometimes sits under a soaked section of insulation several feet wide.

Storm safety steps in the first hour

Stay out from under wet ceilings

A ceiling holding water can release it at once, and wet ceilings near light fixtures raise an electrical concern. Do not climb onto a wet roof, and do not touch downed lines or anything touching them.

  • If a light fixture is dripping or the ceiling is bulging, switch off the breaker for that area if you can reach the panel on dry floor.
  • Place buckets under active drips and move furniture and electronics out of the splash zone.
  • Poke a small hole in a sagging ceiling only if it is safe to stand clear, so trapped water drains in a controlled way.
  • Take photos and short videos of the roof, ceilings and yard from the ground.
  • Call a roofer for a tarp. Crews dry the inside, but they do not handle roof work.

Storm damage versus storm flooding

The line between the two matters for repairs and for insurance. Rain that comes through the roof or wall is storm water damage. Water that rises from the ground, enters at doors and covers the floor from outside is flooding.

According to that city page, flash flooding tends to begin around an inch of rain an hour, and the city operates floodgates at six spots. If water came in from yard level, go to flood damage restoration, because the work and the policy rules differ. If both happened, call and explain, and the crew will sort the areas.

How crews dry a storm-damaged home

  1. 1

    Find the wet areas

    Moisture meters and a thermal camera map the ceiling, walls and insulation, including areas that feel dry.

  2. 2

    Remove what cannot recover

    Soaked insulation and drywall that has sagged or softened are cut out and bagged, which also opens the cavity to air.

  3. 3

    Extract any standing water

    Water on floors is pumped or vacuumed first. Wet carpet is lifted if needed, and emergency water removal covers the technique.

  4. 4

    Bring in the machines

    Fans and dehumidifiers run until meter readings match the dry goal taken from untouched materials.

  5. 5

    Record the work

    Readings and photos are logged for the insurer, and the follow-on work moves to water damage repair.

ANSI/IICRC S500 is the industry reference document for water damage restoration, and crews follow its general approach. It is a reference, not a certification claim. The EPA recommends getting wet materials dry inside 24 to 48 hours to hold mold back, and that clock is why this page asks you to call early.

What changes the cost of storm cleanup

  • How many rooms and ceilings took water
  • Whether insulation, drywall or flooring needs removal
  • The height and layout of the damaged area, which affects access
  • How long the water sat before drying started
  • The number of drying days and equipment pieces the structure needs

A written scope is the most reliable way to compare options. We do not quote dollar amounts over the phone.

Storm damage and your policy

TDI describes most homeowner policies as covering sudden, accidental loss, and a storm hit is normally reported that way. Anything lasting can wait for the insurer to view the damage, so a tarp and drying are the right first moves. Keep receipts and ask your agent how wind and hail terms apply to you.

Mistakes that cost more after a storm

  • Waiting for the adjuster before any drying, which lets mold start
  • Throwing away damaged materials before photographing them
  • Making permanent roof or ceiling repairs before the insurer has looked
  • Climbing a wet roof to inspect it yourself
  • Ignoring the attic, where wet insulation can hide for weeks

A short check of the attic after any hard storm costs nothing and catches many leaks early.
