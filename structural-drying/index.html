Structural Drying and Dehumidification in Colleyville, TX

Drying a house is a measured process, not just running fans. This page explains the steps and gear in plain terms, and the water mitigation page covers the full first response.

☎ Call (817) 623-9267

What structural drying means

Structural drying is the controlled removal of moisture from the frame, floors, walls and ceilings of a building. Water that has soaked into wood, drywall and concrete does not leave on its own at a useful speed. Crews speed up evaporation and then capture the vapor, so the structure returns to a normal moisture level.

North Texas conditions make this a real task. The National Weather Service reports normal annual precipitation at DFW of 37.01 inches for 1991 to 2020, and humid summers slow natural drying. A closed-up house in August can stay damp for a long time without help.

A dry surface does not prove a dry structure. Meters are used because hands cannot tell.

Moisture mapping and thermal imaging

A crew starts by measuring. A pin or pinless moisture meter reads wood, drywall and subfloor, and readings from unaffected rooms set the baseline. A thermal imaging camera shows cooler patches where evaporation or trapped water is lowering the surface temperature.

The camera does not measure moisture directly, so its images are confirmed with the meter. The result is a map that marks where water went and where equipment should go.

Heat, humidity and grains in plain words

Drying depends on the air. Warmer air can hold more vapor, and drier air pulls moisture out of wet materials faster. Crews watch temperature, relative humidity and a number called grains per pound, which counts how much water vapor is in a pound of air.

The goal is to keep the air dry enough that wet materials keep releasing water. The EPA says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent, and Texas A&M AgriLife Extension also gives 30 to 50 percent. Those targets matter because mold needs sustained moisture.

The gear crews bring

  • Air movers. Loud, low fans that sweep air over damp surfaces, and some are tilted to reach the lower wall.
  • LGR dehumidifiers. Low grain refrigerant units that pull vapor from the air and drain the water away.
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers. Units that use a drying wheel and work in cooler or very wet conditions where refrigerant units struggle.
  • Floor drying systems. Mats or panels that pull air through a wet floor assembly, sometimes called injectidry-style drying.
  • Wall cavity tools. Small holes or removed baseboards let warm, dry air move behind drywall.
  • Meters and loggers. Devices that track conditions so the plan can change from visit to visit.

Crews use this equipment, and the type depends on the materials and the room. A tile kitchen and a wood-floor hallway are dried differently.

Drying goals, visits and logs

Each job gets a goal for each material, usually based on what dry readings look like in unaffected parts of the same house. Crews return on later visits to read the meters again, move equipment and adjust the setup. Those readings go into a drying log.

  1. 1

    Assess and map

    Meters and thermal imaging show what is wet and what is not.

  2. 2

    Remove what cannot dry

    Soaked pad, insulation or drywall is removed so air can reach the wet framing.

  3. 3

    Place the machines

    Fans and dehumidifiers go where the map says, and the room is closed to keep the dry air in.

  4. 4

    Monitor

    Readings are logged at each visit and gear is moved or removed as materials reach the goal.

  5. 5

    Confirm dry

    Final readings are compared with the goal before the equipment is picked up.

Drying time depends on the materials, the class of water damage and the conditions in the house. A promise of a fixed number of days would not be honest, because dense hardwood and concrete dry slower than drywall.

Safety steps before and during drying

Electrical and air quality safety

Water and power do not mix, and drying equipment adds new loads to the circuits. Use only circuits that are dry and in good condition, and keep cords out of standing water.

  • Cut the breaker serving any soaked room before anyone steps inside.
  • Do not use a household fan to dry a flooded room if the outlet or cord is wet.
  • If the water came from a sewage source, stop and call before entering, because the materials are contaminated.
  • Keep children and pets away from running equipment and hoses.
  • Open windows only if the weather helps, since humid outdoor air can slow the job.

What changes the cost of drying

  • The square footage and number of rooms that are wet
  • The materials involved, since hardwood and concrete take longer than drywall
  • The class and category of the water, which sets how much material is removed
  • The number and type of machines and how many days they run
  • Access issues such as attics, tight cabinets or tall ceilings

There is no dollar figure in this section because the structure decides the scope. A written plan after the first measurements is the right way to compare.

Drying and your insurance claim

Drying logs, photos and meter readings are the kind of record an insurer may want. TDI notes that home policies typically respond to sudden, accidental water loss and typically not to slow seepage, and it urges owners to report hidden damage promptly. What you can and cannot do yourself

Homeowners can help in small ways. Open cabinet doors, lift furniture off wet carpet, run the air conditioning to pull moisture out and remove soaked rugs. Those steps support drying but do not replace measurement.

A box fan and a bought dehumidifier can handle a small wet spot caught fast. They cannot reach inside walls, cannot read moisture in framing and cannot tell you when the job is finished. Drying that stops too early leaves moisture that comes back as odor, damaged flooring or mold.

ANSI/IICRC S500 is the standard we point to for water damage restoration and drying practice, named here as a reference only. If rebuilding after drying needs permits, the City of Colleyville Building Inspections office handles them at 817-503-1030.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Stopping the equipment because the surface feels dry
  • Closing the house up and running no dehumidification in summer
  • Covering wet floors with new flooring before readings are confirmed
  • Waiting more than a day to start, given the EPA window of 24 to 48 hours
  • Skipping the monitoring visits
